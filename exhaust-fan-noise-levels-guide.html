Exhaust Fan Noise Levels: dB and Sones Explained

Last updated: September 3, 2026

What fan noise numbers actually mean, and how to pick a quiet fan.

Noise is the most common complaint about exhaust fans — and the easiest to get wrong at the specification stage. This guide explains what the numbers mean and how to choose a fan that stays quiet in a bedroom or bathroom.

How Fan Noise Is Measured

Fan noise is usually quoted in dB(A) (decibels, A-weighted to match human hearing) or, in North America, in sones. A change of about 10 dB is perceived as roughly a doubling or halving of loudness. As a practical rule:

Noise levelPerceptionTypical fan
25–35 dB(A)Very quiet — suitable for bedroomsSilent series, EC mixed-flow inline
35–45 dB(A)Noticeable but acceptable in bathroomsStandard ceiling / mixed-flow inline
45–60 dB(A)Clearly audible — kitchens, utility roomsAxial wall / window fans

What Makes a Fan Quiet

  • Impeller design: mixed-flow impellers are inherently lower-turbulence than axial propellers.
  • Motor type: EC (electronically commutated) motors run quieter and cooler than basic AC motors.
  • Mounting: an inline fan inside a duct is quieter than an exposed wall fan — the duct absorbs sound.
  • Duct match: an undersized duct adds airflow noise ("whistle") of its own.

How to Keep a Fan Quiet

  • Match the duct diameter to the fan outlet.
  • Use rigid duct where possible; flexible duct adds turbulence and noise.
  • Mount the fan on isolation to stop vibration transfer.
  • Choose a silent-series or EC-motor model for bedrooms.

Sones vs dB(A): Reading Both Scales

If you sell into North America you will meet sones. Unlike decibels, sones are linear: 2 sones is twice as loud as 1 sone, the way most people actually hear it. A whisper-quiet bathroom fan is around 0.3–0.5 sones; a standard builder-grade unit is 1.0–1.5; a powerful kitchen axial is 3–5.

The conversion is not linear against decibels — sones double roughly every 10 dB. A useful working reference:

SonesApprox. dB(A)What it sounds likeTypical application
0.25~25 dBBarely audiblePremium silent series, bedroom en-suite
0.5~32 dBSoft background humQuiet bathroom, hotel guest room
1.0~40 dBClearly present, not intrusiveStandard residential bathroom
2.0~48 dBObvious, conversation slightly raisedKitchen, utility room
4.0+~58 dB+Loud, short-duration use onlyCommercial kitchen, workshop extract

Note the commercial kitchen row: above 80 dB(A) most jurisdictions require hearing protection and noise monitoring. A specification that ignores this can create a compliance problem for the end client, which is worth raising early in a tender.

How to Read a Noise Figure on a Datasheet

This is where most specification mistakes happen. A fan's published dB(A) is often measured under conditions that flatter it, and two fans quoted at the same number can sound completely different once installed. Three things to check:

  • Measurement distance. 3 m is the convention; 1 m figures look 8–10 dB worse for the same fan. Always confirm which was used.
  • Free-air vs in-duct. Some suppliers quote the motor's free-air noise with no duct attached and no back pressure. Add a real duct run and 50–100 Pa of system resistance and that figure typically rises 5–10 dB.
  • Airflow at which it was measured. Noise rises steeply with speed — a fan rated 32 dB at 200 m³/h may be 45 dB at its maximum 400 m³/h. The honest datasheet states the airflow alongside the noise figure.

BAER measures at 3 m with the fan mounted in a representative duct run at its rated duty point, and we state the airflow that applies. If a competing quote looks implausibly quiet, ask for the measurement conditions — the answer usually explains the gap.

Diagnosing a Noisy Fan After Installation

When a client reports a noisy fan, the fault is usually installation, not the product. Three symptom types cover almost everything:

SymptomLikely causeFix
Low hum or buzz that travels through the ceilingStructure-borne vibration; fan screwed directly to joist or plasterboardAdd rubber isolation mounts or hang the fan on a bracket; never rigid-fix to a joist
High-pitched whistleAir velocity too high — undersized duct, crushed flexible duct, or a restrictive grilleIncrease duct diameter to match the outlet; replace crushed flex with rigid; use a free-area grille at least equal to duct area
Rattle or knocking that comes and goesLoose screw, backdraught shutter flapping, or debris in the impellerTighten fixings; check shutter free movement; clear the impeller
Noise much louder than the datasheetFan working far up its curve against excessive static pressureRe-measure static pressure; reduce duct length or bends, or move to an inline mixed-flow fan with more pressure capability

A rough field check: if the fan sounds right at the grille but loud elsewhere in the room, the problem is vibration. If it is loud at the grille itself, the problem is air velocity.

Frequently asked questions

What is a quiet exhaust fan in dB? ＋
Below 35 dB(A) is considered quiet and suitable for bedrooms; 25–30 dB(A) is genuinely silent in practice. Standard residential bathroom fans sit around 38–45 dB(A). Always check whether the figure was measured at 1 m or 3 m.
How many sones is quiet for a bathroom fan? ＋
0.5 sones or less is quiet enough that most people do not notice it running. 1.0 sone is standard and acceptable for a family bathroom. Above 2.0 sones is loud for a small room.
Why is my new fan noisier than the datasheet says? ＋
Usually one of three things: the figure was measured in free air with no duct resistance (adding 5-10 dB once installed), the duct is undersized so air velocity is high, or the fan is rigid-mounted and transmitting vibration into the structure.
Does a bigger fan make less noise? ＋
A larger fan moving the same airflow is quieter, because it can run slower. That is why oversizing the duct diameter and choosing a larger, slower-running model is a standard noise-reduction strategy.
Are inline duct fans quieter than wall fans? ＋
Yes, typically by 5-10 dB at the room, because the motor sits inside the duct away from the occupied space and the duct itself absorbs sound. This is the standard approach for bedrooms and hotel guest bathrooms.
