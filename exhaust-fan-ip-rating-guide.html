IP Rating Guide for Exhaust Fans: Decode & Verify a Claim

BAER (Zhejiang Baer Electrical Technology Co.,ltd) — factory-direct exhaust and inline duct fans — explains what an IP rating means and how to check the report behind it.

An IP rating on an exhaust fan tells you how well the enclosure resists dust and water. This guide decodes the two digits, compares the ratings buyers most often specify, and shows how to verify a claim before it goes into a submittal.

Last updated: November 23, 2026

IP ratings appear on almost every exhaust fan datasheet, and few numbers are as widely misread. Buyers treat the code as a quality grade, assume a higher number is always better, and rarely ask for the report behind it. None of that helps when a fan sits above a shower, in a grease-heavy kitchen, or on a rooftop in a dust storm.

This guide decodes the digits and focuses on verification, not on assigning one rating to a whole catalogue. A rating belongs to a specific model and configuration, so the useful skill is knowing which questions to ask and what a credible answer looks like. What the two digits mean

IP stands for Ingress Protection, defined in IEC 60529. The first digit rates protection against solids and dust (0–6); the second rates water (0–9). The digits are not a score and are not added together — IP55 is not "ten", and it is not automatically better than IP44 in every situation.

Read the digits separately. In a dusty site the first digit matters most; above a shower or in a washdown area it is the second. A supplier who can say which digit drives your choice understands the specification.

First digit — solid particles

DigitWhat it meansRelevance to a fan
2Protected against solid objects larger than 12.5 mmBasic finger safety; not a dust rating
4Protected against objects larger than 1 mmCommon minimum for indoor fans
5Dust-limited: ingress will not interfere with operationTypical choice for dusty or outdoor sites
6Dust-tight: no dust ingressUsed where fine dust or sand is a real failure mode

Second digit — water

DigitWhat it meansRelevance to a fan
0No protectionIndoor, dry, controlled spaces only
1Protected against vertically falling dropsMinimal condensation exposure
4Protected against splashing water from any directionBathrooms, covered outdoor areas
5Protected against a low-pressure water jet from any directionWashdown areas, exposed outdoor faces
6Protected against a powerful water jetHeavy washdown or marine exposure

The water scale is not evenly spaced: digit 4 is splash, digit 5 is a low-pressure jet, which is why IP44 to IP55 is a bigger step than the numbers suggest. The test applies to a complete enclosure in a defined orientation — a modified cable entry or a missing blanking plug can invalidate it.

Three installation scenarios

The same fan can be specified differently in three rooms: what changes is the environment, not the duty. The sketches below show the detail to ask about, not a rating assigned to any BAER model.

Kitchen — grease, steam and washdown

A kitchen extract fan sits in hot vapour, airborne grease and periodic cleaning with water. The second digit matters most because the enclosure may be sprayed during cleaning and the cable entry can carry moisture into the motor. Grease buildup is a separate maintenance issue: a rating does not make a fan cleanable. Ask how the housing opens and whether the motor enclosure is protected independently of the duct.

Fig. 1 — Kitchen extraction: ask how the motor enclosure and cable entry are protected, and keep the isolator serviceable.

Bathroom — splash zones and humidity

In a bathroom, water direction and installation zone drive the specification more than dust. A fan above a shower sees splash; one in a dry WC area sees mainly humidity. Confirm the second digit rather than relying on the words "bathroom fan" in a title. Continuous humidity also attacks bearings and cable insulation, so the rating is only one part of the durability question.

Fig. 2 — Bathroom: moisture resistance is a water-ingress question; the installation zone decides what to specify.

Dust and sand — the first digit

In desert locations, fine dust gets into motor windings, bearing seats and shutter hinges. Here the first digit is the deciding number: dust-limited protects operation, dust-tight prevents ingress altogether. A shutter that closes firmly when the fan is off is a separate practical detail. Fig. 3 — Dust and sand: the first digit describes solid-particle protection; a tight-closing shutter is a separate detail to ask about.

What a sealed enclosure actually involves

A rating comes from several features working together: housing joint, cable entry, shaft and shutter arrangement, and drainage path. Knowing these four areas makes it easier to judge a claim — and to spot configurations that quietly remove it.

Fig. 4 — Housing joint: a continuous compressed gasket is what a higher first or second digit usually depends on.

Fig. 5 — Cable entry: the enclosure is only as protected as its weakest opening.

Fig. 6 — Shutter and drain: water that gets in must be able to leave; a sealed shaft and a drain path are separate details.

Two practical points: a modified fan is a different fan — drilling a hole or removing a blanking plug can void the tested condition — and water that enters must be able to leave. Neither point shows in a catalogue photograph, which is why the report and an assembly video are worth more than a marketing sentence.

How to verify an IP claim before you order

A credible claim traces to a document naming the tested model and matching the enclosure you are buying. The five steps below work for any supplier.

  1. Ask for the report, not the letters. A datasheet line is a statement; a test report is evidence. Request it before the specification is frozen.
  2. Check the model and voltage. The report must name the exact model, size and voltage in your quotation. A report for a different motor or housing does not cover your fan.
  3. Check the standard and laboratory. The report should name IEC 60529 or the national equivalent and identify the test laboratory. No standard reference means no usable certificate.
  4. Compare the enclosure with the product offered. Check the cable entry, housing screws, shutter and drain. A different entry arrangement needs an updated report.
  5. Keep it in the submittal pack. File the report with the quotation. When the sample arrives, compare its nameplate and enclosure with the report.

Six questions to put in your RFQ

  • Which model, size and voltage was the rating tested on?
  • Which standard and which laboratory issued the report, and what is the report number?
  • Does the rating cover the complete assembled fan, or the motor enclosure only?
  • What cable gland and blanking arrangement is fitted in the tested configuration?
  • Does changing the shutter, motor or voltage change the rating?
  • Can you supply the report with the quotation and reference it on the packing list?

IP is not the whole specification

The IP code is not a substitute for the rest of the technical file. Airflow at a stated pressure, sound level, motor efficiency, insulation class and bearing life must be checked separately. For airflow specifically, our fan performance curve guide explains why a catalogue airflow figure is not the airflow you will measure in a ducted installation.

IP is not interchangeable with other codes. IK covers mechanical impact; NEMA is a North American classification with a different test basis, so direct conversion is misleading. CE, CB and ISO 9001 cover conformity, product safety certification and quality management — none states an ingress rating.

Frequently asked questions

What does an IP rating on an exhaust fan actually tell me?

It states how well the enclosure resists solid particles (first digit) and water (second digit) under IEC 60529 testing. It says nothing about airflow, noise or motor efficiency — treat it as one line in a specification.

Is a higher IP rating always better for a bathroom or kitchen fan?

Not automatically. A tighter enclosure can cost more and needs care with drainage and cable entry. Specify what the zone needs, then check the report covers the exact model and voltage.

What is the difference between IP54 and IP55?

Both are dust-limited on the first digit. The second differs: 4 resists splash from any direction, 5 resists a low-pressure jet. The difference matters in washdown and exposed outdoor areas.

Can I accept a catalogue that lists one IP number for a whole fan series?

Be careful: cable entries, shutters and motor options can change the rating. Ask which model was tested and request the report. One number applied to every variant without a report is not a specification.

How do I check an IP claim from a supplier?

Ask for the test report. Check its number, the model and voltage tested, the standard named (IEC 60529 or equivalent), the laboratory, and whether the enclosure matches the one you are buying.
