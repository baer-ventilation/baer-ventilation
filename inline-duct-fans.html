Inline Duct Fans: The Complete Guide

Last updated: September 7, 2026

How inline duct fans work, how to size one, and where each type performs best — factory-direct from BAER.

An inline duct fan is a fan mounted inside a duct run, so air enters and leaves along a straight line. Unlike a wall or window fan, it can be hidden in a ceiling void or along a duct, and — in its mixed-flow form — it holds its airflow against the pressure of long ducts, bends and grilles. This guide explains the types, how to size one, and where each works best.

How an Inline Duct Fan Works

An inline duct fan sits inside the duct, so air flows straight through it. The motor drives an impeller — propeller (axial), hybrid (mixed-flow) or backward-curved (centrifugal) — that accelerates air along the duct. Because the fan body is inside the duct, the duct itself absorbs most of the sound, which is why inline fans are the quiet choice for bedrooms and offices. The trade-off is that the fan must overcome the duct's static pressure; a weak axial booster will simply stall under resistance while a mixed-flow or centrifugal unit keeps moving air. How to Size an Inline Duct Fan

Sizing has three steps:

  • Calculate required airflow from room volume and air changes per hour — use the CFM calculator.
  • Add margin for resistance: every bend, grille and metre of duct reduces real-world airflow. Add 10–20%.
  • Match the diameter: the fan diameter should equal the duct diameter. Undersized ducts create pressure and noise.
Required CFM = (Room Volume in ft³ × ACH) ÷ 60
or  Required m³/h = Room Volume in m³ × ACH

Worked example: A villa bathroom of 3.5 m × 3 m × 2.7 m = 28.4 m³ at 8 ACH needs 227 m³/h (134 CFM). With a 4 m duct and two bends, add 20% → about 270 m³/h (160 CFM). Choose a mixed-flow inline fan rated above that at the expected static pressure.

Static pressure, explained

Static pressure (Pa) is the resistance in the duct from its length, bends, grilles and filters. A booster axial fan is cheap but collapses past a few Pa; a mixed-flow fan holds airflow to ~30–50 Pa; a centrifugal unit goes higher. Applications

  • Bathroom extraction through a ceiling duct — where a wall fan cannot reach the outside.
  • Kitchen hood / extraction — grease-laden air through a duct run.
  • Long ducted systems in villas, hotels and offices — centralised extraction.
  • Bedrooms and living spaces — where the fan must be heard as little as possible.

Noise & Sound Levels

Inline fans are quieter than wall or window fans partly because the duct muffles them and partly because mixed-flow impellers are designed for low turbulence. Expect roughly 25–35 dBA for residential units and slightly more for high-pressure commercial models. Match the duct diameter to the fan and use rigid duct to keep levels down. Duct Sizing and Layout Best Practices

The duct is half the system, and getting it wrong wastes a good fan. Follow four rules. Keep it short and straight — every metre of duct and every 90° bend costs you airflow and adds noise; route the fan as close to the outlet as practical. Match diameters — the duct bore should equal the fan spigot; a step-down creates a pressure spike and whistling. Use rigid or semi-rigid duct over flexi-foil, which ripples internally and roughly doubles resistance. Insulate ducts that pass through unconditioned spaces so condensation does not drip and the air stays at temperature. For a typical villa bathroom a 100–125 mm (4–5 inch) rigid run of under 5 m performs best, and you should size the fan from the duct's actual resistance, not just the room volume — a long, complex run needs a higher-Pa unit than the free-air CFM alone suggests.

Troubleshooting: Low Airflow and Noise

  • Weak airflow: check for a crushed or kinked flexible duct, a closed damper, or a filter clogged with the fine desert dust common across the Gulf. Clean and re-test before blaming the motor.
  • Whistling: almost always a diameter mismatch or a sharp bend. Re-align the duct to the fan spigot and use a gradual elbow.
  • Vibration through the ceiling: the fan is transferring motion to the structure. Add rubber mounts or a short flexible coupling at the fan body.
  • Fan won't start on a 60Hz grid: confirm the motor is rated for the local frequency — a 50Hz-only unit on 60Hz runs fast and may trip thermal protection. BAER labels every unit clearly for the destination market.

Installation Tips

  • Mount with the arrow pointing in the airflow direction.
  • Use rigid duct where possible — flexible duct adds resistance and noise.
  • Support the fan to avoid vibration transfer to the structure.
  • Leave access for cleaning and maintenance.

Energy Efficiency and Motor Choice

Inline duct fans run for thousands of hours a year, so the motor choice drives most of the lifetime cost. A standard AC motor is cheap and robust and suits intermittent residential use. An EC (electronically commutated) motor integrates the controller into the motor, runs cooler and modulates speed efficiently, typically cutting energy use by 30–60% at part load — the payback is fastest in 24/7 commercial extraction, plant rooms and hotel corridors. For villas that run a fan for several hours a day, EC pays back within a couple of years. Pair any EC or multi-speed AC unit with a thermostat or humidity sensor so it only runs when needed — the single biggest saving after the motor itself. In hot climates like the Gulf, a more efficient motor also means less waste heat dumped into the very space you are trying to ventilate, a compounding benefit during summer peaks. For projects specifying many units, ask BAER for the EC option on the quote; our EC vs AC motor guide works through the numbers for your duty cycle.

Maintenance

  • Clean the impeller and duct interior every 6–12 months — dust is the main cause of lost airflow on Middle East sites.
  • Check the flexible coupling and mounts for wear that can transmit vibration and noise.
  • Verify the capacitor and motor temperature during summer peaks when run hours are longest.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between an inline duct fan and an axial fan?

An inline duct fan mounts inside the duct and, in mixed-flow form, holds airflow against duct pressure. An axial fan moves more air at low pressure but collapses under duct resistance — fine for a short wall pass-through, poor for a long duct run.

How do I size an inline duct fan?

Calculate the required CFM from room volume and air changes per hour, add 10–20% margin for duct resistance, and match the fan diameter to the duct diameter. Our CFM calculator does the maths instantly.

Are inline duct fans quiet?

Yes. Mixed-flow inline fans are generally quieter than axial fans because the duct absorbs sound and the impeller is designed for lower turbulence. Expect around 25–35 dBA for residential units.

Can I use an inline duct fan for a bathroom?

Yes. An inline duct fan is the correct choice when bathroom extraction runs through a ceiling duct rather than a direct wall opening — common in villas and apartments.

Inline Duct Fan Buying Checklist

  • Know the room volume and ACH — bathrooms need about 8 ACH, kitchens 15–20.
  • Measure the duct run — length, bends and grille count set the static pressure you must overcome.
  • Match the fan to the duct diameter and choose mixed-flow unless the run is short and straight.
  • Confirm voltage and frequency for the destination market before ordering.
  • Plan maintenance access so the impeller can be cleaned without dismantling the ceiling.
