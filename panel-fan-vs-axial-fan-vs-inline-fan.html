Panel Fan vs Axial Fan vs Inline Duct Fan: How to Choose

BAER (Zhejiang Baer Electrical Technology Co.,ltd), a China exhaust fan manufacturer since 2003 — CE, CB and ISO 9001 certified, MOQ 100 pcs, samples in 3–7 days — explains panel, axial and inline fan selection below.

Pressure capability decides the specification — not the airflow number in the catalogue.

Last updated: November 24, 2026

Buyers often treat "panel fan", "axial fan" and "inline duct fan" as three competing products. They are not. A panel fan is an axial fan — the same propeller principle — mounted in a square wall plate instead of a cylindrical case. The genuinely useful comparison is between the three ways that principle gets packaged, because that packaging decides how much pressure the fan can generate, and pressure is what decides whether your installation works.

How the Three Families Actually Move Air

The labels describe how the impeller and housing are built, not three unrelated machines. Understanding the mechanism makes the pressure and noise differences predictable, instead of a list of numbers to memorise.

Panel fan — a propeller in a wall plate

A panel (wall or window) fan is an axial propeller mounted in a square flange that sits flush with a wall. Air approaches along the shaft, passes the blades and leaves on the same axis. It is built for volume at almost zero resistance: one 12-inch KLT-20B moves 792 m³/h in free air. The square plate, shutters and simple two-pole motor keep it the cheapest option per cubic metre delivered. Its weakness is pressure — add duct and the free-air number collapses.

Cased axial fan — the propeller in a tube

Put the same axial propeller inside a cylindrical case and it can be fitted into a short duct or wall sleeve. The case directs the flow and gives a little more pressure capability, but the physics is unchanged: high flow, low pressure, and a steep fall-off once system resistance climbs. It occupies the narrow middle ground between a bare wall fan and a true duct fan.

Inline mixed-flow fan — axial in, swirled out

An inline mixed-flow impeller adds a radial component to the axial flow. Air enters along the shaft but leaves at an angle, so the fan develops real static pressure while keeping a compact cylindrical housing. That pressure is what makes a 10-metre duct run work. It costs more because the impeller, casing and motor bearings are all specified for a harder duty.

Why Pressure Is the Deciding Factor

Airflow and pressure are a trade-off, and every fan sits somewhere on that curve. A wall panel fan is optimised for volume: a 12-inch KLT-20B moves 792 m³/h with almost nothing in its way. Put ten metres of duct in front of it and the same fan may deliver a fraction of that, because the duct resists the flow and the fan cannot generate the pressure to overcome it.

An inline mixed-flow fan accepts a lower free-air figure in exchange for the pressure to push air through real ductwork. That is why a hotel with a central ceiling void uses inline fans with short grille drops, while a warehouse with an open wall uses panel fans and no duct at all.

The 10-Metre Duct-Run Trap

The most common selection failure is choosing on free-air airflow and ignoring the duct. A wall fan rated 792 m³/h with nothing attached may deliver less than half that behind 10 metres of duct and two bends, because every metre of straight duct and every elbow adds resistance. The fan has to generate enough pressure to overcome it — and a panel fan simply cannot.

Rule of thumb from installers: a panel fan is acceptable direct-through-wall or up to about 3 metres of straight rigid duct, with 15–30% allowed off the free-air rating. Beyond roughly 5 metres, or with two or more bends, move to an inline fan. If the run has filters, a hood or a long vertical rise, the resistance climbs again — size from the curve, not the catalogue headline.

Noise: Where the Fan Sits Matters More Than the dB Number

A dB figure in a catalogue is usually measured at the fan inlet in a laboratory. What a room occupant hears depends mainly on distance and barriers. Moving the fan out of the occupied space is the single most effective acoustic measure — more effective than shaving a few decibels off the motor.

For a hotel bathroom or a bedroom, the quiet specification is an inline mixed-flow fan above the ceiling with a short flexible connector. A panel fan in the wall always radiates into the room, no matter how low its stated dB. Flexible mounts and a correctly sized grille keep the duct from transmitting structure-borne noise.

When to Use Both

Extract and make-up air are two halves of one system. A single panel fan extracting from a sealed workshop will underperform; the same fan with a matched inlet louvre at the opposite end will move dramatically more air for the same energy. On larger projects the usual arrangement is panel fans for extract and either louvres or a second fan for supply, sized so the two roughly balance.

Selection is not only about performance on day one. The three families differ in how they are installed, how easily they are cleaned, and how long they stay quiet — all of which affect lifetime cost more than the purchase price.

A panel fan in a dusty workshop will accumulate blade dust, lose airflow and grow noisy unless it is cleaned. An inline fan hidden in a ceiling void is quieter but must be accessible for service — specify an access panel at order stage. Whatever the family, an undersized fan running at the wrong point on its curve is the fastest route to noise and premature bearing failure.

Common Mistakes

  • Buying an inline fan for a job a panel fan would do. If the wall is external and the run is short, ducting adds cost and pressure for nothing.
  • Buying a panel fan for a long duct. The more expensive mistake of the two, because the installation fails after it is built in.
  • Comparing free-air figures across families. A panel fan's 800 m³/h and an inline fan's 500 m³/h are not comparable numbers — compare them at the working pressure of your installation.
  • Ignoring noise location. A panel fan puts the noise in the room. An inline fan puts it in the ceiling void. For hotels and bedrooms that difference decides the specification.
  • Forgetting the shutter. An open wall fan is a hole in the building. On a panel fan the auto-shutter is standard; on a ducted system you need a separate back-draft damper.

A Worked Selection Example

Two buyers both need 500 m³/h. Buyer A extracts directly through an external kitchen wall — no duct. A KLT panel fan is the correct call: lowest cost, easiest install, and the free-air rating is nearly the delivered airflow. Buyer B needs the same 500 m³/h in an internal bathroom with an 8-metre duct and two bends to the facade. The panel fan would stall; size an inline mixed-flow fan from its curve at the estimated system pressure, allow for the bends, and mount it in the ceiling void for quiet operation. Same airflow target, different family — that is the whole decision in one example.

What to Tell Your Supplier for an Accurate Quote

Specify the duty, not just a model name, and a manufacturer can match the family to the job. Give: required airflow (m³/h), total duct length and number of bends, mounting position (wall, window, duct or ceiling void), supply voltage and frequency, acceptable noise level, and destination for certification requirements. With those inputs a supplier can recommend a panel, cased axial or inline model and send the matching performance curve.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a panel fan the same as an axial fan?

A panel fan is a type of axial fan. Both use a propeller to move air along the axis of rotation. The difference is the housing: a panel fan is built into a square wall plate for flush wall or window mounting, while a cased axial fan sits in a cylindrical housing for duct or sleeve mounting.

Which is better, an axial fan or an inline duct fan?

Neither is better in general. An axial fan moves more air at low pressure and costs less, so it suits direct wall mounting and short runs. An inline mixed-flow duct fan generates more pressure, so it suits longer duct runs and installations where the fan must sit away from the room.

Can I use a panel exhaust fan with ducting?

Yes, up to roughly three metres of straight rigid duct, and you should allow 15 to 30 percent of the free-air rating for the loss. Beyond about five metres, or with two or more bends, an inline duct fan is the correct choice.

Why is an inline duct fan more expensive?

It has a more complex mixed-flow impeller, a proper casing and usually better motor and bearing specification to cope with the higher pressure duty. You are paying for pressure capability and for the ability to put the noise away from the occupied room.

What is the quietest option for a hotel bathroom?

An inline mixed-flow duct fan mounted in the ceiling void, with a short flexible drop to the ceiling grille. Moving the fan out of the room is the single most effective noise measure; a panel fan in the wall always radiates its noise into the bathroom.

How much airflow do I lose in a long duct?

It depends on the fan's pressure capability and the duct layout, not a fixed percentage. As a planning allowance, a wall panel fan behind up to three metres of straight rigid duct may lose roughly 15–30% of its free-air rating; at ten metres with bends the loss can be far larger. An inline mixed-flow fan holds its airflow much better. Always confirm against the model's pressure–flow curve.

When should I choose a centrifugal fan instead?

If system resistance is very high — long industrial duct networks, filters, dust collection or tall vertical risers — a centrifugal fan develops the pressure that even an inline mixed-flow fan cannot. It is larger and costlier, so for typical bathroom, kitchen, workshop and ceiling-void extraction an inline mixed-flow fan is the usual answer. Send your duct layout to the supplier and let them size it.
