What is CFM and why it matters

CFM (cubic feet per minute) is the amount of air an exhaust fan moves in one minute. In metric markets — the Middle East, Europe and Australia — the same figure is usually quoted as m³/h (cubic metres per hour). Both measure the same thing: how fast the fan can replace the air in a room.

The single biggest reason an exhaust fan "doesn't work" is that it was sized for the room's floor area instead of its volume and the air changes per hour (ACH) the application requires. A 50 CFM fan that is adequate for a small bathroom will do almost nothing in a 500 m³ restaurant kitchen.

The formula

CFM = (Length × Width × Height) × ACH ÷ 60

For metric: m³/h = Room volume (m³) × ACH. To convert, 1 CFM ≈ 1.699 m³/h.

Worked example: bathroom

A bathroom measuring 10 ft × 8 ft × 8 ft (volume 640 ft³) needs 8 air changes per hour:

CFM = 640 × 8 ÷ 60 = 85 CFM (≈ 145 m³/h)

You would select a fan rated at least 90–100 CFM after adding a small margin for the duct run.

Two things the calculator can't see

  • Duct resistance (static pressure): every bend, grille and long run reduces real-world airflow. Add 10–20% to the calculated CFM, or use a fan with a steeper pressure curve (an inline or mixed-flow fan rather than a basic axial fan).
  • Filters and shutters: backdraft shutters and insect screens reduce airflow. Size up rather than down.

How to calculate the CFM of an axial fan

An axial fan is rated at a specific test pressure, and the number on the datasheet is measured at a set static pressure — often 0 Pa, called free air. The real CFM you get depends on the resistance of your installation:

  • Free-air rating: a fan advertising 984 CFM may deliver that only with no duct, no shutter and no grille attached.
  • Operating point: real airflow is where the fan's pressure curve crosses your system's resistance curve — typically 20–40% below the free-air number for a wall installation with a shutter.
  • Practical rule: if you must have 500 CFM at the blade, spec a fan whose free-air rating is 650–700 CFM, or choose a mixed-flow inline fan whose curve holds up under pressure.

This is why our product pages list CFM at rated pressure, not just free air — see the exhaust fan range for tested values per model.

Whole house fan size calculator

For whole-house ventilation the goal is 15–20 air changes per hour in hot climates (a shortcut rule is 30 CFM per 100 sq ft of floor area). Calculate the total volume of the conditioned space, then apply the same formula with a higher ACH:

Whole-house CFM = Total volume (ft³) × 15 ÷ 60

Example: a 2,000 sq ft house with 9 ft ceilings has a volume of 18,000 ft³, so it needs 4,500 CFM of continuous extract. In practice this is delivered by several fans zoned by area — bathrooms and kitchens on their own controls — rather than one oversized unit. For hot, humid Gulf climates, run the fans continuously at low speed; see our Middle East climate ventilation guide.

Wall vs ceiling fan: does the CFM change?

The required CFM stays the same — air changes depend on room volume, not where the fan sits. What changes is how much of the rated CFM you actually get:

  • Wall / window fans exhaust straight outside with almost no duct loss, so you get close to the rated CFM. They are the first choice for kitchens, workshops and ground-floor rooms — see the full comparison in our wall vs ceiling exhaust fan guide.
  • Ceiling fans are ducted through the roof space, adding bends and resistance. Add 15–20% to your CFM target, or step up one model size.

Verify real airflow after installation

For commercial projects, consultants increasingly ask for ventilation verification — proof that the installed system delivers the design airflow. A simple commissioning check anyone can run:

  1. Measure air velocity at the grille with an anemometer, averaged over a 5-point grid.
  2. CFM = average velocity (fpm) × free grille area (sq ft).
  3. Accept values within ±10% of the calculated design. If measured airflow is lower, check shutter drag, duct leaks and backdraft flaps first.

Frequently asked questions

What does CFM mean for an exhaust fan? ＋
CFM stands for cubic feet per minute and measures how much air a fan moves in one minute. A bathroom exhaust fan typically needs 50–80 CFM, while a kitchen or workshop needs several hundred CFM depending on room size and air changes per hour.
How do I convert CFM to m³/h? ＋
Multiply CFM by 1.699 to get m³/h — for example, 100 CFM ≈ 170 m³/h. To convert m³/h back to CFM, divide by 1.699.
How many air changes per hour does a bathroom need? ＋
A bathroom typically needs 8 air changes per hour (ACH), a residential kitchen 15, and a commercial kitchen up to 20. Warehouses usually require 6 ACH.
How do I calculate CFM for a whole house fan? ＋
Multiply the total volume of the conditioned space (length × width × ceiling height, in feet) by 15–20 air changes per hour, then divide by 60. A 2,000 sq ft house with 9 ft ceilings needs about 4,500 CFM of continuous extract.
Is a wall exhaust fan better than a ceiling exhaust fan? ＋
A wall fan exhausts directly outside with minimal duct loss, so it delivers close to its rated CFM — ideal for kitchens and workshops. A ceiling fan is quieter and hidden but typically loses 15–20% of airflow to duct resistance, so size up one step if you choose ceiling mounting.
