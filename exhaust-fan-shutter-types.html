Exhaust Fan Shutter Types Explained

Last updated: September 10, 2026

Gravity, motorised, fixed louvre or weather hood — and why the hinge pins are what actually fail.

An exhaust fan is a hole in the building envelope. When it stops, that hole is still there — and what comes through it is dust, sand, insects, rain, noise and the neighbour's cooking. The shutter is the part that closes the hole, and it is the specification most buyers leave blank. This guide covers the four ways the problem is solved and which one suits which climate.

Why the Shutter Matters More Than It Looks

  • Back-draft. Without a shutter, air from a shared shaft or a neighbouring room flows back into yours whenever your fan is off.
  • Dust and sand. In Gulf and desert installations, wind-driven sand will find an open wall opening. It settles on the impeller and unbalances it.
  • Insects and birds. An open 200 mm hole in a wall is an entry point.
  • Rain. Wind-driven rain through a wall fan causes damp staining and, over time, corrodes the motor.
  • Heat loss and draughts. In European and North American installations the shutter is a comfort and energy measure, not a hygiene one.
  • Noise. A closed blade set attenuates outside noise. An open hole does not.

Type 1: Gravity Auto-Shutter

The blades are hinged and held closed by their own weight. When the fan runs, the airflow pushes them open; when it stops, gravity closes them again. No actuator, no wiring, nothing to fail electrically.

  • Advantages: no power, no controls, no extra cost on most panel fans, fails closed rather than open.
  • Limitations: the hinge pins are the weak point — dust and grease make the blades stick. It relies on airflow to open, so it adds a small pressure loss. It is not a rain seal and not an airtight seal.
  • Best for: wall panel fans in bathrooms, kitchens, workshops, warehouses and agricultural buildings. Standard on the KLT series.
  • Maintenance: clean the hinge pins every three months in dusty or coastal locations. A shutter that stays open is almost always a dirty pin, not a broken blade.

Type 2: Motorised Shutter

The blades are driven open by a small motor or a spring-return actuator, linked to the fan's control circuit. The blades open positively before or as the fan starts and close positively when it stops.

  • Advantages: opens reliably even when airflow is low; can be interlocked with a BMS; closes positively against a light seal.
  • Limitations: an extra powered component and an extra failure mode. Needs wiring and a control signal. Higher cost.
  • Best for: larger fans where airflow alone may not lift the blades, installations on a BMS, and projects where the client requires a positive close signal for commissioning.

Type 3: Fixed Louvre or Open Grille

Angled fixed blades, no moving parts. Cheap and robust, but it does not close — it only breaks the line of sight and sheds some rain.

  • Advantages: nothing to maintain, no pressure loss from a moving blade set, lowest cost.
  • Limitations: does not stop back-draft, dust or insects.
  • Best for: continuously running industrial extract, or where a separate damper is fitted elsewhere in the system.

Type 4: External Weather Louvre or Hood

A separate component fitted on the outside face of the wall. It is not an alternative to the fan's shutter — the two are normally used together.

  • What it adds: protection from wind-driven rain, a bird and debris screen, and a tidier external appearance.
  • When it is essential: exposed facades, coastal sites with salt-laden wind, and any installation where rain can be driven horizontally against the wall.
  • Note: a hood adds pressure loss. Allow for it when sizing — see the sizing guide.

The Most Common Failure, and How to Avoid It

Shutters fail open far more often than they fail closed. The cause is almost always contamination on the hinge pins — sand in the Gulf, grease in a kitchen, salt in a coastal installation. The blades then hang slightly open, which produces three symptoms at once: visible dust ingress, a continuous draught, and a measurable loss of airflow because the blades are sitting in the air stream.

The fix is a maintenance interval matched to the environment, not a different shutter. On a dusty site, clean the pins monthly rather than quarterly.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an auto-shutter on an exhaust fan?

It is a set of hinged blades held closed by their own weight. Airflow from the running fan pushes them open, and gravity closes them when the fan stops. It needs no power and it fails closed, which is why it is the default on wall panel fans.

Do I need a shutter on an exhaust fan?

If the fan discharges through an external wall, yes. Without one the opening stays open when the fan is off, letting in dust, insects, rain and back-draft from neighbouring spaces, and losing heat in colder climates.

Why does my exhaust fan shutter stay open?

Almost always contamination on the hinge pins — sand, dust or kitchen grease. Clean the pins and check that the blades swing freely. A shutter that stays open also reduces airflow, because the blades sit in the air stream.

Is a gravity shutter enough for a coastal installation?

It handles dust and back-draft, but it is not a rain seal. On an exposed or coastal facade, fit a gravity auto-shutter for daily operation and an external weather hood for wind-driven rain, and expect to clean the hinge pins more often because of salt.

Can I add a shutter to a fan that does not have one?

Not usually as a field modification — shutters are designed into the fan housing. If you need a closing blade set, specify it at order stage, or fit a separate back-draft damper in the duct. On the KLT series the auto-shutter is standard on every model.
