Exhaust Fan Troubleshooting: Not Working, Noisy or Moving No Air

A practical diagnostic guide from BAER Ventilation (Zhejiang Baer Electrical Technology Co.,ltd) — factory-direct since 2003. Work through the symptom, isolate the cause, and know when to stop and call a professional.

Most exhaust fan complaints trace to a short list of causes: power, a restriction in the duct, something loose, or a worn motor. This guide separates the three big symptoms — no sound, too much noise, and no airflow — and gives you a 14-row check table to work through in order.

Last updated: November 23, 2026

Safety first. Isolate power at the breaker before removing a cover or touching wiring. If you smell burning, see sparks, have a breaker that keeps tripping, or need to measure live voltage, stop and call a qualified electrician. Never work on a connected fan.

A fan that fails in service is rarely a mystery once you separate the symptoms. The three questions below route you to the right section. The figures quoted for noise are the same measured values used on our noise levels guide, so the two pages read consistently.

Before you start: a few minutes of preparation

Three quarters of troubleshooting is observation. Before you take anything apart, spend a few minutes recording what the fan was doing. This prevents the common mistake of replacing a part that was never faulty, and it gives a supplier enough information to help remotely.

  • Identify the exact model. The rating plate on the motor or housing carries the model and the voltage/frequency. A KLT panel fan and an inline duct fan share almost no parts, so the model determines which checks are relevant.
  • Note when the symptom began. A fault that appeared on day one usually points to installation or a shipping issue. One that appeared after months of correct operation points to wear, dust buildup or a component at the end of its life.
  • Describe the sound in plain words. Rattle, hum, grind, squeal and whistle each map to a different cause in the table below. A short phone recording is often more useful than an adjective.
  • Establish the baseline. If there is an identical fan nearby that works correctly, compare the two. The same model in the same room gives you a control for both sound and airflow.
  • Have the right tools ready. A screwdriver, a torch and a soft brush cover most mechanical checks. A multimeter and any test that opens the electrical enclosure belong to a qualified electrician.

One useful first test is to run the fan in free air with the grille removed, if the model allows it safely and the power is isolated during removal. If airflow returns when the duct is disconnected, the fan itself is healthy and the restriction is in the duct system. That single observation redirects the whole diagnosis and avoids an unnecessary motor replacement.

Symptom 1 — The fan is completely silent

Start at the supply and work toward the motor. A silent fan means no electricity is reaching it, or the motor circuit is open. Confirm each step before moving to the next.

  1. Confirm the breaker and any switch. A tripped breaker, a switched-off isolator or a failed wall switch is the simplest explanation.
  2. Verify power at the terminal. This is a job for a qualified electrician if it requires opening the enclosure or testing live conductors.
  3. Listen for a hum. A faint hum with no rotation means power is present but the motor cannot start — commonly a failed run capacitor or a seized bearing, not a dead supply.
  4. Check for a safety cut-out. Some fans have a thermal or access interlock that must be reset or closed.

Symptom 2 — Noise or vibration appeared

New noise is almost always mechanical and local. A rhythmic rattle is something loose; a grinding or squeal that grows over weeks is bearing wear. Use the measured dB context from our sones-to-dB table: a standard bathroom fan sits around 38–45 dB(A), and a jump well above that after installation is a sign to investigate.

SoundLikely causeWhat to check
Rattle / buzzLoose screw, shutter flap or debrisIsolate power, tighten mounts, clear the impeller
Grinding / squealWorn bearingBearing is usually sealed — the motor typically needs replacement
Hum louder than beforeRigid mounting transmitting vibrationUse rubber mounts or an anti-vibration kit
WhistleHigh air velocity in a small or restricted ductIncrease duct diameter, shorten the run, remove tight bends

Vibration is worth separating from pure airborne noise. If you feel a buzz through the wall or ceiling but the sound at the grille is modest, the fan is transmitting its vibration into the structure through rigid fixings. Anti-vibration mounts or a short flexible connector break that path. If the noise is loud at the grille itself, look at air velocity: a duct that is too small for the airflow makes a whistle that no amount of mounting will fix.

Most modern exhaust fans use sealed, permanently lubricated bearings with no oil port. Adding oil is not a valid repair and can damage the winding. A bearing that grinds or squeals has reached the end of its service life and the motor is normally replaced as a unit. The exception is a fan under warranty — in that case do not open the motor at all, because dismantling usually voids the claim.

Symptom 3 — It runs but moves no air

If the impeller spins freely but you cannot feel airflow at the outlet, the fan is probably fine and the installation is restricting it. Work downstream from the fan: grille, filter, damper, duct, terminal.

One cause is frequently overlooked: make-up air. A fan cannot remove air from a room unless replacement air can enter. In a tightly sealed bathroom or utility room, the fan may spin perfectly and produce almost no flow because the room goes slightly negative and resists extraction. Open the door or a transfer grille and retest — if airflow suddenly returns, the duct and fan are fine and the room needs a deliberate air inlet path.

Finally, confirm that the fan is actually exhausting to outside rather than into a ceiling void or wall cavity. A duct that has come off its terminal fitting can leave the room feeling stuffy even though the fan runs and the grille vibrates. Check the external outlet while the fan is running; you should feel clear airflow leaving the building.

Repair or replace?

A loose screw, a blocked filter or a crushed duct is a fix you can make today. A sealed bearing, a burnt motor winding or a repeated earth fault is not a practical field repair — replacement of the motor or unit is usually the safer and more economical choice. If the fan is still under warranty, contact the supplier before opening anything.

SituationPractical decision
Loose mount, dirty grille, shut damperFix on site — no parts usually needed
Failed capacitorSmall, low-cost part — electrician can replace
Worn sealed bearingMotor replacement is normally the realistic option
Burnt winding / earth faultReplace the motor or unit; do not reuse
Duct undersizedFix the installation, not the fan

A useful rule of thumb is to weigh the cost of the part against the remaining service life of the fan. A capacitor or a shutter is a small expense on a unit that is otherwise sound. A motor replacement on an older fan often costs enough that a new, correctly sized model — with a current warranty and better efficiency — is the better decision. Installed cost matters too: if reaching the fan requires dismantling a ceiling or a duct run, paying once for a reliable replacement is usually cheaper than paying twice for a sequence of small parts.

Preventing the problem from coming back

Most of the faults above are installation or maintenance issues, not product defects. A short routine prevents the majority:

  • Clean the grille and filter on a schedule — dust buildup is the leading cause of weak airflow.
  • Check that the shutter opens and closes freely at least once a season.
  • Keep the duct run short, sealed and free of sharp bends; use the correct diameter.
  • Use anti-vibration mounts where the fan fixes to a lightweight panel or ceiling.
  • Size the fan to the room from the start — an undersized fan run at full speed is noisier and wears faster.

If you are choosing a replacement, the airflow calculation guide covers sizing, and the performance curve guide explains why the rating on the box is measured at one duty point rather than every installation.

Frequently asked questions

Why is my exhaust fan not pulling air?

The most common causes are not the motor. Check in order: a shut or blocked damper/shutter, a clogged grille or filter, a crushed or kinked flexible duct, a duct run that is too long or narrow, and a missing make-up air path. If the impeller spins freely but little air moves, the restriction is almost always downstream of the fan.

Why is my exhaust fan making a loud noise suddenly?

A new rattling or buzzing usually means something loose or rubbing: a mounting screw, a shutter flap, debris in the impeller, or a fan touching its housing. A grinding or squealing that develops over weeks points to bearing wear. Isolate power and inspect before the problem escalates.

Why did my exhaust fan stop working completely?

Work from the supply backwards: verify power at the outlet and the breaker, check any wall switch or pull-cord, then test whether the motor hums (power present, motor not starting — often a capacitor or seized bearing) or is completely silent (no power or open circuit). Electrical tests beyond a visual check should be done by a qualified electrician.

Can I oil an exhaust fan bearing?

Most modern exhaust fans use sealed, permanently lubricated bearings — there is no oil port, and adding oil will not help. If a sealed-bearing fan is grinding or squealing, the motor normally needs replacement. Check your model's manual before assuming the bearing is serviceable.

How do I know if the duct is too restrictive?

Signs include weak airflow at the outlet despite a spinning impeller, higher noise than the datasheet, and a fan that works when tested in free air but not once installed. Shortening the run, using a larger diameter, and eliminating tight bends all reduce resistance. Compare against the airflow guide for your room size.

When should I stop and call an electrician?

Immediately, if you notice a burning smell, sparks, a tripping breaker, discoloured wiring, or a hot motor housing. Also call a professional for any test that requires opening the electrical enclosure or measuring live voltage. Never work on a connected fan.

Still stuck?

If you have worked through the matrix and the fault remains, note the exact model, the voltage and frequency, when the symptom started, and what you observed at each step. That information lets a supplier or electrician help without a guess.
