Exhaust Fan Supplier Audit Checklist: 30 Points to Check Before You Order

BAER (Zhejiang Baer Electrical Technology Co.,ltd) — legal entity registered 2017, factory heritage since 2003, CE / CB / CCC / ISO 9001 documented — publishes the same 30-point checklist we use on our own production.

An exhaust fan supplier audit turns a claim into evidence before money moves. This checklist scores 30 points in six groups and names the photos and documents that prove each one.

Last updated: November 23, 2026

An exhaust fan supplier audit is not a factory tour with a camera. It is a structured check that turns a claim — "we are a manufacturer", "we hold CE", "we make 50,000 fans a month" — into evidence you can verify before an order is placed. Most bad orders start in the gap between a trading company catalogue and a factory floor: the wrong impeller, a certificate issued for a different model, or a nameplate that fails customs clearance.

This checklist gives you 30 scored points in six groups, the eight photos to demand from the production floor, and ten red flags that should stop a deal. It is the same list BAER applies when we assess our own sub-suppliers. Run it in person, over a live video call, or by requesting documents first — the sequence matters less than getting a written answer for every line.

Why a supplier audit protects your order

A fan is a configured product: its performance depends on parts you cannot see in a catalogue photograph. Two suppliers can offer the same 200 mm panel fan and deliver completely different airflow because one winds its own motors and the other buys generic ones. An audit establishes which one you are talking to.

Certificates are the second reason. A CE report, a CB test report or a CCC certificate is issued against a specific model, voltage and standard. A scan on a website proves only that the file exists. The audit line is not "do you have CE" but "what is the report number, which model does it cover, and can you show the original with the same nameplate". That question separates a factory that tests from one that downloads.

Entity alignment matters too. A buyer who pays an account held by a different company from the one on the quotation has no contractual claim against the factory. Check the legal name, the Unified Social Credit Code and the bank beneficiary as one set. BAER publishes both codes on its verification page, and states 2017 as the legal entity registration date while keeping 2003 as factory heritage.

The 30-point supplier audit scorecard

Score each line 0 (no evidence), 1 (verbal answer only) or 2 (document or on-camera proof). A factory confident in its own production usually scores 50 or more out of 60. Below 35, keep the order at sample stage. The groups are ordered so that identity and certificates come before capacity — a supplier who cannot pass the first ten points is not worth testing further.

Ask for photos taken in one session, with a visible date or a handwritten model reference. Stolen stock images usually fail this test because lighting, floor and wall differ between shots.

Ten red flags that mean stop

One red flag is a question. Three in the same audit is a pattern. Do not let a low offer move the order forward while these remain open.

  1. The legal name on the proforma invoice differs from the business licence.
  2. A certificate is shown as a screenshot with no number or issuing body.
  3. The quoted model does not appear anywhere on the test report.
  4. A supplier claims to be a factory but the video never leaves the showroom.
  5. The quotation drops sharply while the specification stays identical.
  6. Bank details change by email, with no voice or video confirmation.
  7. The testing laboratory is described only as "an independent lab".
  8. The supplier claims every certification, including ones unrelated to fans.
  9. Every audit line is answered "yes" without a single document attached.
  10. Only catalogue photos are offered; nothing is shown in production.

None of these proves bad intent on its own. They do mean the burden of proof has moved to the supplier. A factory used to audits answers them with files, not with reassurance.

What ISO 9001, 14001 and 45001 actually cover

A supplier who lists three ISO standards without explaining them is asking for credit they may not have earned. The three cover different systems, and only one of them is a quality-management certification.

StandardCoversWhat it does not prove
ISO 9001:2015Quality management: controlled processes, traceability, corrective action.It does not certify that a specific fan model passes a safety test.
ISO 14001Environmental management: waste, emissions and resource use.It says nothing about product quality or airflow performance.
ISO 45001Occupational health and safety: protection of workers on site.It is not a product standard and does not apply to the fan itself.

Read the certificate number and scope, not the logo. BAER currently holds ISO 9001:2015 only — certificate 04625Q15304R1M, issued by Beijing Head International Certification Co., Ltd. (CNAS C046-M) and valid to 2028-11-16. This page does not claim ISO 14001 or ISO 45001 certification. Our certifications guide explains which documents apply to product approval in each market.

How to audit without flying to China

Start with documents. Ask for the licence, the certificate list with numbers and validity dates, an annual capacity statement and the model drawing before you book a call. A supplier who cannot send these in one email will not produce them live either.

Then run a live walk-through with a fixed route: gate and sign, moulding, winding, assembly, test bench, packing. Ask the person holding the camera to stop at each station and show a nameplate or a work order with your model code. A recorded marketing video proves nothing about the line running today.

Finish with the scorecard sent back in writing, including the lines marked TBC. When the sample arrives, compare its nameplate, shutter and carton marks with those answers — this closes the loop between the audit and the delivered product. Frequently asked questions

Can I audit a supplier without visiting China?

Yes. Ask for a live video walk-through of the production floor, not a recorded clip, then cross-check the legal name, certificate numbers and USCC against the documents. Request that the camera show the nameplate and a certificate original in the same shot. An in-person visit is better, but a structured video audit catches most trading-company misrepresentation.

What is the difference between a factory and a trading company?

A factory owns the production equipment and can show you moulding, winding, assembly and testing on one site. A trading company can source a good fan but cannot change the motor winding or the impeller tooling. Neither is wrong, but the quotation and the audit must say which one you are dealing with. Ask for the legal entity name on the licence and compare it with the bank beneficiary.

Which certificate numbers should I check?

Ask for the certificate number, the issuing body and the validity date for each claim, then verify at the issuer or the government platform. For BAER, ISO 9001:2015 is 04625Q15304R1M (CNCA, valid to 2028-11-16), CCC is 2024010702612682 (CQC, valid to 2029-03-11), and the CE EMC report is XH2601E0321945GC from XHIT. Check that the model on the report matches the fan you are buying.

Does BAER hold ISO 14001 or ISO 45001?

No. BAER currently holds ISO 9001:2015 only. This page explains what the three standards cover so you can read a supplier's claim correctly; it does not claim certification BAER does not hold. If a project requires 14001 or 45001, tell us at the RFQ stage and we will answer in writing.

What should I do if the supplier refuses a factory video?

Treat it as an unanswered audit line, not proof of fraud. Ask what they will provide instead: dated production photos, a test-bench readout with the model visible, or a third-party inspection report. If every request is declined, keep the order at sample stage until you have independent evidence.

How often should I re-audit a supplier?

Re-check the certificate validity dates before each production run, and repeat the full 30-point audit once a year or after any change of legal entity, factory address or bank account. Certificate expiry is the most common silent failure — a model approved two years ago may no longer be covered by the report on file.
