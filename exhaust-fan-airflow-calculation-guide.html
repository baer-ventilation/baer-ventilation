How to Calculate Exhaust Fan Airflow: The Complete CFM & m³/h Sizing Guide

Exhaust fan airflow is one formula: required airflow (m³/h) = room volume (m³) × air changes per hour. A 13.5 m³ bathroom at 8 ACH needs 108 m³/h — specify a fan rated 120–130 m³/h to allow 10–20% for duct resistance and aging. The one calculation that decides whether your ventilation works — with ready-to-use tables and worked examples.

Last updated: October 20, 2026

The most common reason a ventilation system underperforms is not the fan's quality — it's the size. An undersized fan never clears the room; an oversized one wastes energy and creates noise and drafts. This guide shows the standard calculation used by HVAC engineers, with tables you can apply directly.

Step 1: Measure the Room Volume

Room volume (m³) = Length (m) × Width (m) × Height (m)

Example: a bathroom 2.5 m × 2 m × 2.7 m = 13.5 m³.

Step 2: Choose Air Changes per Hour (ACH)

ACH is how many times per hour the entire room's air is replaced. Standards by application:

ApplicationRecommended ACH
Residential bathroom8–10
Kitchen (domestic)15–20
Commercial kitchen hood exhaust30–60 (by hood design)
Bedroom / living room4–6
Home office / retail6–8
Workshop / light industrial8–12
Warehouse4–6
Hot climates (Gulf region rooms)10–15 (higher for heat control)
Laundry room10–12
Parking garage6–8

Step 3: Apply the Formula

Required airflow (m³/h) = Room volume (m³) × ACH

Worked example — the bathroom from Step 1, at 8 ACH:

13.5 m³ × 8 = 108 m³/h required

Add a 10–20% margin for duct resistance and aging: target a fan rated 120–130 m³/h.

Step 4: Convert Between m³/h and CFM

Suppliers quote both units. Conversion:

The Calculation That Most Buyers Forget: Static Pressure

Catalog airflow is measured at free air (0 Pa). Real installations have ducts, bends, grilles and shutters that create resistance:

  • Direct wall/window mount (no duct): nearly full catalog airflow — see the wall vs ceiling comparison for the full loss table
  • 1–2 m of duct with one grille and backdraft shutter: expect 10–20% airflow loss
  • Long duct runs (5 m+) with multiple bends: 30–50% loss with axial fans — this is where inline mixed-flow duct fans become necessary
Rule of thumb: If your installation involves real ducting, either size up one fan class or switch from axial to a mixed-flow inline fan. Request the fan's performance table showing airflow at 50/100/150 Pa, not just the free-air headline number.

Reading the table on a real job

A workshop 5 m × 4 m × 3 m is 60 m³. At 10 ACH that is 600 m³/h required. Free air, KLT-20B at 792 m³/h looks like more fan than the job needs. Put it on a 5 m duct run instead and the −40% column gives 475 m³/h — short of 600. The honest answer on that job is not a bigger panel fan; it is a BXF inline unit, which is quoted at working pressure rather than free air.

This is the decision most quotes get wrong. A panel fan is chosen for its free-air number and then installed on ductwork, where the number no longer applies. If the run is longer than one wall thickness, price the inline range before you price the panel range — the inline versus axial comparison has the pressure ratings side by side.

Full Worked Example: Gulf-Region Apartment

A bedroom 4 m × 3.5 m × 2.8 m (39.2 m³) in a hot climate requiring 12 ACH:

  • 39.2 m³ × 12 = 470 m³/h
  • Plus 15% margin for duct + shutter resistance = 540 m³/h target
  • Recommended: two window/wall fans of ~280 m³/h each, or one ceiling inline system

Common Sizing Mistakes

  • Sizing by floor area instead of volume — ceiling height changes everything
  • Ignoring the backdraft shutter — adds 5–10 Pa resistance on every cycle
  • Choosing one big fan for multiple rooms without duct design — the far room gets almost nothing
  • Using free-air ratings for ducted installations — see static pressure above
  • Forgetting make-up air — a fan cannot extract from a sealed room; there must be an inlet path (door undercut, grille, window vent)

How to Check an Airflow Claim Before You Order

Airflow is the easiest specification to quote loosely, because the same fan produces very different numbers depending on where it is measured. Four questions separate a usable figure from a sales figure.

  • Free air or at pressure? Ask for the static pressure the figure was measured at. 1,200 m³/h at 0 Pa and 1,200 m³/h at 380 Pa describe two different machines, and the second is worth far more on a ducted job.
  • Which report, and which models? Ask for the test report number and check that the model series you are buying actually appears in it. A report covering one series does not transfer to another, even from the same factory.
  • Convert the units yourself. One m³/h is 0.589 CFM, so a “1,000 CFM” claim is 1,699 m³/h. Suppliers quoting whichever unit flatters the comparison are usually quoting the other one on the datasheet.
  • Match the nameplate to the order. Every BAER unit leaves the assembly bench with a plate carrying its model and serial, so the fan on site can be traced back to the batch it came from.

If you are comparing us against another quote, send both datasheets and we will state which figure is free air and which is at pressure. That comparison is usually more useful than the price difference. Our own documentation and entity records are listed on the verification page.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate exhaust fan airflow (CFM) for a room? +
Multiply the room volume by the required air changes per hour (ACH), then divide by 60. For a 20 ft x 15 ft x 10 ft room (3,000 ft3) at 8 ACH: 3,000 x 8 / 60 = 400 CFM. In metric, airflow (m3/h) = room volume (m3) x ACH.
What ACH (air changes per hour) should an exhaust fan deliver? +
Residential bathrooms need 8 ACH; commercial kitchens and humid Gulf-climate spaces need 10-15 ACH; warehouses and factories typically 6-10 ACH based on heat and pollutant load. Round up to the nearest standard fan size.
How do I convert CFM to m3/h (and back)? +
1 CFM is about 1.699 m3/h. Multiply CFM by 1.7 to get m3/h; divide m3/h by 1.7 to get CFM. BAER specs list both units - for example the 6-inch shutter fan delivers 984 CFM (about 1,672 m3/h).
Should I add extra CFM for ducts, elbows and filters? +
Yes. Budget roughly 10-15% loss per 90-degree elbow, 1-2% per metre of straight duct, and 10-20% for filters. Add 20-30% headroom to the calculated CFM so the installed fan still meets the target after system losses.
How do I size an exhaust fan for a warehouse or factory? +
Size by heat and contaminant load, not just volume. Use CFM = (heat load in watts x 3.16) / temperature rise in C for heat removal, or a dilution formula for pollutants. For most industrial bays plan 6-10 ACH and confirm against process heat with our engineers.
