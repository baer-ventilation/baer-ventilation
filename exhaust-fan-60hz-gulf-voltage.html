60Hz Exhaust Fans: What Gulf Buyers Need to Specify

Last updated: September 10, 2026

Saudi Arabia is the only 60 Hz market in the Gulf — and the one where a 50 Hz fan fails in the field.

Most exhaust fan enquiries we receive do not mention frequency. They mention voltage, or nothing at all. For every Gulf market except one, that is fine — but the exception is the largest market in the region, and it is the one where a 50 Hz fan will fail in the field. This guide explains what changes at 60 Hz, which countries are affected, and what to put in your purchase order.

The Short Version

Saudi Arabia runs on 60 Hz. Every other Gulf state runs on 50 Hz. A fan built for 50 Hz and installed on a 60 Hz supply does not simply run slightly faster — it runs about 20% faster, demands roughly 1.7 times the shaft power it was designed for, and its motor cannot supply that at the same voltage. The result is overheating, nuisance tripping, and a motor that fails long before its rated life.

If your project is in Saudi Arabia, your purchase order must state 220 V / 60 Hz. A 60 Hz fan uses a different motor build — it is a separate production batch, so it affects both the minimum order quantity and the lead time. Tell us at enquiry stage, not at shipping stage.

Why Frequency Matters So Much

An AC induction motor's speed is set by the supply frequency, not by the voltage. Synchronous speed is:

n = 120 × frequency ÷ number of poles

A four-pole motor turns at 1,500 r/min on a 50 Hz supply and 1,800 r/min on 60 Hz. That is a 20% increase — and a fan does not respond to speed in a straight line. The fan affinity laws say:

QuantityRelationship to speedAt +20% speed
AirflowProportional to speed+20%
Static pressureProportional to speed squared+44%
Shaft power requiredProportional to speed cubed≈ +73%

So the fan asks for roughly 1.7 times the power. Meanwhile, at the same 220 V, the 60 Hz supply gives the motor a lower volts-per-hertz ratio than it was designed for — the magnetic flux in the core falls and the torque the motor can produce drops. The fan wants more power and the motor can deliver less. That gap becomes heat in the windings.

What the installer sees: a fan that runs noticeably faster and louder, a motor casing that gets uncomfortably hot, a thermal protector that trips after a period of running, and eventually a burnt-out winding. Because it works on day one, the fault is usually only discovered months later — after the building is handed over.

You cannot fix this with a voltage regulator or a dimmer. Reducing the voltage reduces torque further and makes the overheating worse. The only correct answer is a motor wound and rated for the supply frequency.

What to Put on the Purchase Order

  • Voltage and frequency, always together. "220 V" is an incomplete specification.
  • Destination country and city. It tells us which frequency and plug standard to build for.
  • Plug type. The Gulf uses Type G (BS 1363). Either specify the plug or specify "no plug, for hard wiring" — do not leave it open.
  • Quantity by model. A 60 Hz motor is a separate batch, so the MOQ applies per model per frequency.
  • Whether 50 Hz and 60 Hz units are needed in one order. Mixed-frequency containers are possible but must be planned — tell us early.

How to Read a Fan Nameplate

The rating plate on the motor states the voltage, frequency, current and speed the motor was designed for. Before installing anything, check three things against the site supply:

  • Frequency — 50 Hz or 60 Hz. This is the one that causes failures.
  • Voltage range — for example 220–240 V, which covers the Gulf 230 V and 240 V systems.
  • Current — compare the rated current against what the circuit is protected for, and against what the motor actually draws on site.

If the nameplate says 220 V / 50 Hz and the site supply is 220 V / 60 Hz, stop. The fan is the wrong build for that market.

Planning Around the Extra Lead Time

Because a 60 Hz unit is a different motor batch rather than a stock item, Saudi projects should be planned with a longer window than a Kuwait or UAE project of the same size. The practical steps are to confirm the frequency at enquiry stage, to consolidate all 60 Hz models into one order so the batch is economic, and to avoid adding 60 Hz models to an order after production has started.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which Middle East countries use 60Hz?

Among the Gulf states, only Saudi Arabia. Kuwait, Qatar, the United Arab Emirates, Oman, Bahrain, Yemen and Iraq all use 50 Hz. Elsewhere, the United States, Canada, Mexico, Brazil, the Philippines, South Korea and Peru are 60 Hz markets.

Can I use a 50Hz exhaust fan on a 60Hz supply?

It will run, but about 20 percent faster and it will demand roughly 1.7 times the shaft power it was designed for. The motor cannot deliver that at the same voltage, so it overheats, trips its thermal protection or fails early. Always specify a motor rated for the supply frequency.

What happens if I run a 50Hz fan at 60Hz?

Speed rises by about 20 percent, which increases airflow by about 20 percent and the power required by about 73 percent. The motor casing runs hot, thermal protection may trip, and the winding can burn out. The fault often appears months after installation rather than immediately.

Does Saudi Arabia use 50Hz or 60Hz?

Saudi Arabia uses 60 Hz. The GCC Standardization Organization's low-voltage regulation rates the Saudi supply at 127/220 V at 60 Hz, and 220 V single phase is the practical figure in buildings. It is the only 60 Hz market in the Gulf.

What voltage and frequency should I specify for a Gulf project?

State the destination country and we will confirm it, but as a rule: Saudi Arabia 220 V 60 Hz; Kuwait, Qatar and Yemen 240 V 50 Hz; United Arab Emirates, Oman and Bahrain 230 V 50 Hz; Iraq and Jordan 220 to 230 V 50 Hz. Always give voltage and frequency together, never voltage alone.
