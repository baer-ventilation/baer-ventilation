Exhaust Fans: The Complete Buyer's Guide

Last updated: September 7, 2026

Every exhaust fan type BAER manufactures, how to size one, and how to choose the right model for your application.

An exhaust fan removes stale, humid or contaminated air from a room and discharges it outside. It is the simplest and most cost-effective way to control moisture, odour, heat and indoor air quality — and it is also the most commonly mis-specified piece of ventilation equipment. This guide covers every exhaust fan type BAER manufactures, how to choose the right one, and how to size it correctly.

How Exhaust Fans Work

Every exhaust fan is a simple machine: an electric motor spins an impeller that creates a pressure difference, pulling air from the room and pushing it out through a duct or grille. The room then draws in replacement air through gaps, windows or a dedicated intake, creating continuous ventilation. The difference between fan types comes down to where the impeller sits (in the ceiling, wall, window or duct) and how it handles resistance (static pressure). A fan that moves plenty of air in free air can still fail if it cannot push against a long duct — which is exactly why BAER offers both low-pressure window fans and high-pressure mixed-flow duct fans in one catalogue.

How to Choose the Right Exhaust Fan

Four factors decide which fan fits. Get these right and the fan will perform; get any of them wrong and even a good fan will disappoint.

1. Airflow (CFM / m³/h)

Size the fan to the room's volume and the required air changes per hour (ACH), not to floor area. Use our CFM calculator to get the number, then add a 10–20% margin for duct resistance.

2. Static pressure

Ducts, bends and grilles create resistance. A wall axial fan collapses under it; an inline mixed-flow fan holds its airflow. If ducting is involved, choose for pressure, not just airflow.

3. Noise

For bedrooms and living spaces, choose a low-noise model and match the duct diameter to the fan. See our exhaust fan noise levels guide for what to expect.

4. Voltage and frequency

Match the motor to the destination grid: 110V/60Hz for North America, 220–240V/50Hz for Europe and most of the Gulf, and 220V/60Hz for Saudi Arabia.

Sizing Your Exhaust Fan Correctly

Under-sizing is the #1 cause of "my fan doesn't work" complaints. Size to volume and air changes per hour (ACH), never to floor area. A room with a 2.5 m ceiling holds twice the air of one with a 1.25 m ceiling at the same footprint — and you have to move all of it. Use the formula below or the CFM calculator.

Required CFM = (Room Volume in ft³ × ACH) ÷ 60
or  Required m³/h = Room Volume in m³ × ACH

Worked example: A 4 m × 3 m × 2.5 m bathroom = 30 m³. At 8 ACH (typical for bathrooms) that is 240 m³/h, or about 141 CFM. Add 15% for duct and grille resistance and you need a fan rated around 275 m³/h (160 CFM). BAER's 6-inch models deliver 984 CFM at free air and hold strong airflow under pressure, leaving comfortable headroom for real installs. For a step-by-step walkthrough with more room types, see our airflow calculation guide.

Understanding static pressure

Static pressure is the resistance the fan must overcome, measured in Pascals (Pa). Every metre of duct, every bend, every grille and every filter adds Pa. A window axial fan may be rated at near-zero Pa; a mixed-flow inline fan is built to hold its airflow at 20–50 Pa and beyond. If your installation has any ducting, choose for pressure, not just free-air CFM — the number on the box is measured with no duct attached, so real-world airflow is always lower. Budget a 10–20% margin and you will not be disappointed.

Applications

  • Bathroom: 8 ACH — a ceiling or window fan removes steam and prevents mould.
  • Kitchen: 15–20 ACH — a wall or high-pressure inline fan handles grease, smoke and heat.
  • Warehouse / workshop: 6–10 ACH — a large wall shutter or axial fan for high volume.
  • Commercial / hospitality: silent inline or cabinet fans for continuous, quiet operation.

Common Exhaust Fan Mistakes

  • Sizing to floor area, not volume. Always use volume (length × width × height).
  • Buying on free-air CFM. Real installs lose 20–40% to resistance — size with margin.
  • Wrong voltage for the market. A 50Hz fan on a 60Hz grid runs 20% fast and may fail. Match 110V/60Hz (North America), 220–240V/50Hz (Europe, most Gulf) or 220V/60Hz (Saudi Arabia).
  • Ignoring noise in living spaces. A loud fan gets switched off. For bedrooms choose a silent model and a matched duct diameter.
  • No intake path. An exhaust fan cannot move air out if replacement air cannot get in. Provide a gap or intake grille.
  • Skipping maintenance. Dust on the impeller silently cuts airflow; a twice-yearly clean restores performance.

Motor Types: AC, EC and DC

Exhaust fan motors come in three flavours. AC motors are the workhorse — cheap, robust and available at every voltage we stock. EC (electronically commutated) motors integrate the controller into the motor, run cooler, modulate speed efficiently and cut energy use by 30–60% at part load — ideal for 24/7 commercial extraction. DC motors dominate in low-voltage and battery-backup scenarios. For duct fans specifically, our EC vs AC motor guide explains the payback maths. BAER offers AC as standard and EC on request for OEM and large projects.

Exhaust Fan vs Inline Duct Fan: Which to Choose

A "standard" exhaust fan mounts on a wall, window or ceiling and pushes air straight out. An inline duct fan sits inside the duct and is the right call when the air has to travel. Rule of thumb: if the outside is directly behind the fan, a wall or window fan is simplest and cheapest; if you need to route extraction through a ceiling void, across a room or to a distant grille, choose inline. The two are complementary, not competing — many villas use a window fan in the kitchen and inline fans in the bathrooms.

Installation & Maintenance

  • Mount level and seal the duct connection to stop leaks and condensation.
  • Keep the duct short and straight — every bend is lost airflow and added noise.
  • Clean the impeller and grille every 6–12 months; dust build-up cuts airflow and raises motor temperature.
  • Check the backdraft damper opens freely so air flows one way only.

Frequently Asked Questions

What size exhaust fan do I need?

Calculate the room volume (length × width × height) and multiply by the required air changes per hour, then divide by 60 to get CFM. Bathrooms need about 8 ACH, kitchens 15–20 ACH. Use the CFM calculator for an instant result, and add 10–20% for duct resistance.

What is the difference between a wall and ceiling exhaust fan?

A ceiling fan mounts in the ceiling and ducts to the outside; a wall fan mounts through a wall opening. Ceiling fans suit bathrooms and offices; wall fans suit kitchens and workshops where a direct wall path exists.

Do you manufacture exhaust fans for 60Hz markets?

Yes. We supply genuine 220V/60Hz motors for Saudi Arabia and 110V/60Hz for North America, in addition to 220–240V/50Hz for Europe and the wider Gulf.
