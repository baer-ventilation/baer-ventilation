THE BASICS

The Inch Is a Mounting Size, Not an Airflow

On a bathroom or panel fan, the inch refers to the nominal fan diameter inside a square wall panel — a 6 inch panel fan has a 200×200 mm faceplate. On a duct fan, the industry quotes the duct diameter in millimetres instead (100/125/150/200/250/315 mm). On a high-airflow wall shutter fan, the inch describes the blade and housing, and the same nominal size can be built with a much deeper blade and a faster motor. Airflow is set by blade design, motor speed and static pressure — never by the inch alone.

ROOM COVERAGE

Which Size for Which Room

Bathroom up to 5 m²

About 10 m³ of air at 8–10 changes per hour: 80–100 m³/h. A 4 inch (100 mm) quiet bathroom fan is enough — going bigger mainly buys noise.

Bathroom 6–10 m²

150–200 m³/h. 6 inch bathroom fan, or a KLT-10B panel fan if the wall is masonry and you want a shutter louvre.

Kitchen 10–15 m² (domestic)

12–15 air changes: 400–550 m³/h. 8 inch panel fan (KLT-12B, 380 m³/h) as a minimum; use the ducted BXF-150 (620 m³/h) if the fan cannot sit on an outside wall.

Living room / bedroom 20–30 m²

6–8 air changes: 350–600 m³/h. 8 inch panel fan or a silent cabinet fan where noise matters more than volume.

Small office / shop 40–60 m²

650–900 m³/h. 12 inch panel fan (KLT-20B, 792 m³/h) or two smaller units on opposite walls.

Workshop / warehouse 60–100 m²

8–12 air changes: 1,800–3,000 m³/h. 18 inch panel fan (KLT-30B) or two 14 inch units (KLT-25B, 1,500 m³/h each).

THE CALCULATION

Size by Air Changes, Not by Guesswork

Every size decision reduces to one sum: room volume × air changes per hour = airflow needed. Volume is length × width × ceiling height, not floor area — the single most common mistake. Air change rates by room type:

RoomAir changes per hourNotes
Bathroom / WC8–10Higher if there is no window
Domestic kitchen12–1515–20 if frying regularly
Living room / bedroom6–8Comfort, not moisture control
Office / shop6–8Add for occupancy above one person per 10 m²
Workshop / warehouse8–1212–15 where there is welding or solvent
Dishwash / laundry15–20Moisture-driven; use a ducted fan

Worked example: a kitchen 4 × 3 m with a 2.9 m ceiling is 34.8 m³. At 15 air changes that is 522 m³/h — between the 8 inch panel fan (380) and the 6 inch duct fan (620). Take the larger of the two if the fan is ducted or the room has a window that stays shut. Conversions: 1 m³/h = 0.589 CFM; multiply m³/h by 0.59, or divide CFM by 0.59.

MEASURING

Measure the Opening, Not the Old Fan

Three dimensions decide whether a replacement fits, and only one of them is the size printed on the box. The panel is the visible faceplate that covers the wall. The cut-out is the hole you actually make. The outlet is the spigot diameter, which matters if a duct connects to it.

SAME SIZE, THREE FANS

Why One 6 Inch Fan Moves 150 m³/h and Another 1,670

Bathroom fan — 4″ to 6″

Quiet, low power, short duct. Built to shift 50–220 m³/h with an internal backdraught flap for a duct of 100–150 mm. The right choice when the limit is noise, not airflow.

Panel fan (KLT) — 6″ to 18″

Square wall fan with an automatic shutter louvre. Same nominal size, several times the airflow because the blade is deeper and the motor is bigger: 150 m³/h at 6 inch, 1,800 m³/h at 18 inch.

Duct fan (BXF) — 100 to 315 mm

Mixed-flow impeller for pressure, not just volume: 290–2,200 m³/h and up to 693 Pa. Choose it when the fan sits away from the wall or the run has bends.

High-airflow wall shutter — one size, one job

Our 6 inch industrial shutter fan is built for workshops: 984 CFM / 1,670 m³/h at up to 180 Pa. Same 6 inch label, industrial blade and motor.

RULES OF THUMB

Five Things Buyers Get Wrong on Size

1. Using floor area instead of volume. A 3 m ceiling holds 20% more air than a 2.5 m one. Multiply by height, always.

2. Buying one size up instead of fixing the duct. A fan with a long, narrow duct loses more airflow than the next size up would gain. 100 mm duct above 290 m³/h runs at 10 m/s and turns airflow into noise.

3. Forgetting make-up air. Every cubic metre out needs a way back in. A sealed room with a 1,000 m³/h fan can lose a third of that airflow and pull combustion gases down the flue.

4. Oversizing a bathroom. Above about 10 air changes you get a draught across the room, more noise, and a fan that pulls against the door seal. Match the size to the volume.

5. Ordering by inch across borders. A 6 inch fan is 150 mm in a duct, 200×200 mm on a panel and 152 mm on a US spec sheet. Quote both the inch size and the cut-out when you compare suppliers.

BUYER FAQ

Frequently Asked Questions

What size exhaust fan do I need for a 10 m² bathroom? +
A 10 m² bathroom with a 2.6 m ceiling holds 26 m³. At 8–10 air changes that is 210–260 m³/h, so a 6 inch (150 mm) bathroom fan rated around 220 m³/h. If there is no window, take the higher figure. A 4 inch fan is right for a small WC of 3–5 m², not for a family bathroom.
Is a bigger fan always better? +
No. Oversizing causes a draught across the room, more noise, and in a sealed room it starves itself of make-up air so the extra capacity is never delivered. Size to the air-change rate and, where the load varies, use a two-speed or speed-controlled fan instead of a larger one.
My wall opening is 160×160 mm — which fan fits? +
That is the KLT-10B cut-out. Its 200×200 mm panel covers the opening and its 85 mm spigot takes a 100 mm duct — 150 m³/h, 35 dB. If you need more airflow through the same wall, keep the hole and use the BXF-100 duct fan in the run instead, at 290 m³/h.
Why is a 6 inch fan sometimes 150 m³/h and sometimes 1,670? +
Because the inch describes the mounting or blade diameter, not the motor or the blade depth. A 6 inch bathroom fan has a small backward-curved impeller and a low-power motor; our 6 inch industrial shutter fan has a deep axial blade and a much larger motor. Compare airflow at a stated static pressure, not the inch.
How do inches compare to the millimetres on duct fans? +
4″ = 100 mm, 6″ = 150 mm, 8″ = 200 mm, 10″ = 250 mm, 12″ = 300 mm, 14″ = 350 mm, 16″ = 400 mm, 18″ = 450 mm. Duct fans are quoted by duct diameter, so a 150 mm BXF is the “6 inch” one — but it delivers 620 m³/h, four times a 6 inch bathroom fan.
Can I fit a ducted fan if I only have a 100 mm hole? +
Yes — the BXF-100 is 290 m³/h at up to 150 Pa, but 100 mm duct runs at about 10 m/s at that airflow, so keep the run under 3–4 m with no more than two bends. Longer runs need a 150 mm duct and a reducer at the wall.
What if the wall cannot be cut at all? +
Use a window fan: the KLT-W window series covers 468–984 m³/h and mounts in the frame or a replacement glass panel, with the same shutter louvre. It is the usual answer for rented premises and heritage buildings.
Do sizes change for 60Hz or 110V? +
The physical size and cut-out stay the same; the airflow changes. A 60Hz motor runs about 20% faster, so the same fan moves proportionally more air and makes more noise. Quote the voltage with the size so the performance figures we send match what you will install.
